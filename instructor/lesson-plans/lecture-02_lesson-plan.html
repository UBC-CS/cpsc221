Lesson plan — What Does It Do?

The sensation we are going for

Students should feel, in their own mouths, that a sentence they were confident about does not survive being said out loud — and then watch the room build a correct one together in twenty minutes, from nothing but conversation.

The lesson is not “you all failed” and it is not “AI will ruin you.” The arc is generous on purpose:

  1. Individually, almost nobody writes a precise description.
  2. Through conversation, almost everybody gets there.
  3. Therefore: the conversation was the thing that worked.

That last step is the argument for being in the room, and it is demonstrated rather than asserted — which matters, because attendance is not required in this course and the lesson must not quietly contradict that.

Why this slot

Friday of week 1 is the only in-person Friday of the term, and it follows the introduction to running-time analysis. The same function appears in lecture 1 with its name blanked out; here the question is asked live, answered casually, and then handed straight back to them.

It also lands before HW1 and Examlet 1 in week 3. Habits form in week 1.

Budget twelve minutes to reach example 1 and keep it moving — none of it is new material except the last line of the running-times slide, which is the hinge: before we can say how long an algorithm takes, we have to say what it does.

Do not ask “how long does it take?” on that slide. The 2024 deck does, but it is Monday’s opening — asking it Friday telegraphs that the description is the hard part.

That leaves the activity about 38 of its 50 minutes. If it runs tight, cut from the breaking phase, not from the group argument.

The function

The same one from lecture 1, name still blank:

int ____________ (vector<int> arr, int q) {
    for (int i = 0; i < arr.size(); i++)
        if (arr[i] == q)
            return i;
    return -1;
}

It is deliberately trivial. Nobody can hide behind complexity, so the only thing that can fail is the precision of their words.

A description that survives everything below:

Given a vector of integers arr and an integer q, return the index of the first element of arr equal to q, scanning from index 0 upward. If no element equals q, return -1.

Timing (50 minutes)

Starting from example 1, i.e. twelve minutes into the class:

Time Phase Notes
0:00–0:03 Ask it live, take answers “Somebody said: it finds the key”
0:03–0:08 Write, silent, alone Hold the silence
0:08–0:09 Groups of 3, hold up fingers Compliance check, collect nothing
0:09–0:10 Everyone reads aloud at once ~30s, whole room
0:10–0:18 Share and adjudicate in groups “Whose, if any, is correct?”
0:18–0:30 I try to break them Use real statements from the room
0:30–0:36 Build one together on the board From the room’s own words
0:36–0:40 “What just happened” The convergence point — the real lesson
0:40–0:46 Generate the AI description live Recognizing ≠ producing
0:46–0:50 The contract, and close “Say that more precisely”

Protect the group phase and the board-building. If you are running long, cut the number of descriptions you break, not the time groups spend arguing.

The three moves that make this work

1. Simultaneous reading, not round-robin

Everybody reads their own statement aloud at the same time, whole room, about thirty seconds. This is better than taking turns for four reasons: no performance anxiety among strangers in week 1, every single student actually does it, no confident voice anchors the group before others have spoken, and it is fast.

The purpose is for the speaker to hear themselves, not for anyone else to listen. Say that explicitly — “nobody is listening to you, that’s the point.”

Tell them twice not to fix the sentence as they read it. Students patch their own wording mid-sentence automatically, half a second before they reach the gap, and it destroys the entire effect. Naming it is the only defence.

Afterwards, ask “did it sound like you expected?” and wait. Laughter and groans here are the lesson landing.

2. An adjudication test, not a vote

“Whose is correct?” needs a criterion or groups just pick the one that sounds nicest:

Could a stranger reimplement this function correctly, from these words alone?

3. You play the pedantic genie

Take real statements from the room and satisfy them legally but wrongly. Playful, not punitive — you are a malicious implementer, not a judge.

Their words What you hand back
“finds the key in the list” Returns true. I found it.
“returns the position of the key” [5,3,5], key 5 → returns 2. Also a position.
“returns the index of the key” Key absent → returns 0. You didn’t say.
“returns where the key is” A 1-based index. You didn’t say.
“searches for the key and returns it” Returns 5. You said return it.
“returns the index of the key, or -1” Returns any matching index. You never said first.

That last row is the important one: it catches students who were already being careful, so nobody in the room gets to feel exempt.

Watch for: transcription

Someone will narrate the code instead of describing it — “it loops from 0 to size, and if arr[i] equals q it returns i.” That technically passes the reimplementation test, which makes it a good ninety seconds if it comes up naturally: it tells you what the code says, never what it is for, and it is no shorter than just reading the code.

The giveaway is the blank in the function name. A transcription cannot name the function; a real description hands you the name almost for free.

Keep this in your pocket rather than on a slide — take it only if a student raises it.

The naming payoff

“Find” was never enough — a function called find could return a bool, a value, or an iterator. A precise description yields indexOfFirst / firstIndexOf almost automatically. Use this to fill in the blanked-out name.

The AI moment — generate it live

Paste the function into whichever tool you like, in front of them, and let the room watch it produce a clean, correct description in about three seconds. Live is far better than a screenshot: it is more honest, it removes any suspicion of cherry-picking, and the speed is the point.

It will be correct. Do not pretend otherwise — the lesson does not depend on AI being wrong, and students will disengage instantly if you overstate it.

The line that matters:

You are nodding, because it looks right. You would have nodded twenty minutes ago too — before you knew what you were missing.

Then: recognizing a correct answer and being able to produce one feel identical from the inside, and only one of them is what an examlet tests.

The closing contract

Three people who just succeeded together agree on one shared phrase:

“Say that more precisely.”

Aimed at the practice, not the relationship — so it costs nothing personal, requires no exchange of contact details, and works on a partner, a TA, a future study group, or yourself.

Then start using it from the front of the room constantly, all term. Heard in week 1 and then every week after, it stops being an event and becomes how this course talks.

On the optional contact swap

Offer it, then say “or nothing at all” clearly and move on fast.

In a room this size there will be a few students with real reasons not to hand their number to two people they met twenty minutes ago. If the whole room is exchanging while you watch, declining becomes conspicuous — naming the opt-out out loud is what makes it free.

Collect nothing, build no roster, and do not ask next week who is still meeting. Any follow-up makes the opt-out visible again.

Known risks

  • Some students finish writing in ninety seconds. They are usually the ones about to discover they were imprecise. Hold the full five minutes.
  • A group agrees immediately and goes quiet. They accepted the first statement without testing it. Nudge: “read me the shortest one and I’ll tell you how I’d break it.”
  • Tone in the breaking phase. Pedantic genie, not judge. If it reads as mockery in week 1, you lose volunteers for the term.
  • Do not add an attendance pitch at the end. The students in the room just lived the argument. Saying it out loud a second time cheapens it.
