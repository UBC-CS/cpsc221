Algorithm analysis

Analysis of an algorithm gives insight into

  • whether it is correct
  • how long it runs — time complexity
  • how much memory it uses — space complexity


And it lets us compare two algorithms that solve the same problem without running either one.

Thirty seconds. This is a framing slide, not a content slide.

Where we stopped

If an algorithm is not correct, is its value 0?


No — and the exceptions are a whole field each:

  • approximation — provably close, much faster
  • heuristics — usually good, no promise
  • randomized — correct with high probability
  • restricted inputs — correct on the data you actually have

We spent Wednesday on this. Land the answer and move – do not re-run the discussion, it already happened.

Measuring the value of algorithms

Two algorithms solve the same problem. Which is better?

  • Use different metrics — time, space most common
  • Express running time as a (positive) function of input size:

\[T(n) = \text{time to complete the operations on data of size } n\]

  • The form of that function depends on the structure of the algorithm

Running times

Suppose a computer executes \(10^{12}\) operations per sec.

operations n=10 n=100 n=1000 n=10,000 n=\(10^{12}\)

\(n^2\) \(10^{-10}\) s \(10^{-8}\) s \(10^{-6}\) s \(10^{-4}\) s \(10^{12}\) s

So before we can say how long an algorithm takes, we have to be able to say what it does — precisely enough that the question even has an answer.

The last line is the hinge. It is the only sentence connecting the lecture to the activity, so do not rush it.

Algorithmic analysis — example 1

    for (int i = 0; i < arr.size(); i++)
        if (arr[i] == q)
            return i;
    return -1;
}

What does it do?

THIS IS THE START OF THE ACTIVITY. Ask it out loud and take two or three answers. You are fishing for something casual and confident – “it finds the key”, “it searches the array”. Do not correct anything; just accept them and move on. The looseness is the setup.

Any C++ mysteries in the code, clear them here – but keep it short.

Do NOT ask “how long does it take?” today. That is Monday’s opening, and asking it now gives away that the description is the hard part.

Write it down

Your turn — on your own, in silence

Describe what this function does.

The rules

  • Complete sentences. No code, no pseudocode.
  • Someone who has never seen this function must be able to reimplement it correctly from your words alone.
  • Give it a name. Fill in the blank.

Hold the silence. Five minutes feels long and some will finish in ninety seconds – let them sit with it rather than moving on early. The students who finish fast are usually the ones about to discover they were imprecise.

Groups of three

First: hold up fingers

How many written statements can you see in your group?


I am not asking whether they are any good.

Only whether all three of you have one.

Quick scan of the room. This is a compliance check that costs ten seconds and requires collecting nothing. Groups police themselves.

Now read it out loud — all of you, at once

Not one at a time. Everybody, simultaneously, right now.

Nobody is listening to you. That is the point.

Read exactly what you wrote

Do not fix it on the way out of your mouth.

You are going to want to. You will see the gap about half a second before you reach it, and your instinct will be to quietly patch it as you speak.

Resist that. You are reading it so that you can hear it.

Let the whole room read at once – it takes about thirty seconds and the wall of sound is worth having. Two hundred people saying different sentences about the same four lines of code makes the point before anyone discusses anything.

Reading simultaneously does three things a round-robin cannot: it removes all performance anxiety in week 1 among strangers, it guarantees every single student actually does it, and it means no confident voice anchors the group before the others have spoken.

The instruction not to self-repair is the important one. Say it twice. Students patching their own sentences mid-read is automatic and it is exactly what destroys the effect.

Did it sound like you expected?


Now share them with your group.

Ask this and genuinely wait. A few laughs and groans here are the lesson landing – that is the sound of people hearing their own vagueness.

Do not let them revise before sharing. The group needs the original.

Then: whose is correct?

The test

Could a stranger reimplement this function correctly, from these words alone?

Whose passes?

Any of them?

Circulate. Listen for the argument – the groups doing this properly get loud. Silent groups usually accepted the first statement without testing it.

Useful nudge if a group is stuck agreeing: “read me the shortest one, and I’ll tell you how I’d break it.”

Let me try to break them

I will take your words completely literally

  • “It finds the key in the list.” → I return true. I found it.
  • “It returns the position of the key.” → [5, 3, 5], key 5. I return 2. Also a position.
  • “It returns the index of the key.” → Key isn’t there. I return 0. You didn’t say otherwise.
  • “It returns where the key is.” → I return a 1-based index. You didn’t say otherwise.
  • “It searches for the key and returns it.” → I return 5. You said return it.

Use real statements from the room wherever you can – ask for volunteers to read theirs out, and break those instead of these. These are the fallback.

Keep the tone playful. You are being a pedantic genie, not a judge.

And the one that catches the careful people

“It returns the index of the key, or -1 if the key is not in the list.”

That is a good sentence. It handles absence. It says index.

[5, 3, 5], key 5. I return 2.

You never said first.

This is the one that gets the strong students, and it is worth landing carefully – the room should feel that carefulness alone was not sufficient.

Now let’s build one that survives

Together

(on the board)

Build it live from the room’s own words. Take pieces from different groups so the final sentence visibly belongs to everyone.

A version that survives all the attacks above:

Given a vector of integers arr and an integer q, return the index of the first element of arr equal to q, scanning from index 0 upward. If no element equals q, return -1.

Then the name falls out almost for free: indexOfFirst, firstIndexOf, findFirstIndex. Point out that “find” alone was never enough – a function called find could return a bool, a value, or an iterator.

If somebody has instead transcribed the code line by line (“it loops from 0 to size, and if arr[i] equals q it returns i”), that technically passes the reimplementation test, which makes it a great 90 seconds: it tells you what the code says, never what it is for, and it is no shorter than just reading the code. The giveaway is that a transcription cannot name the function.

Recognizing is not producing

Reading a correct answer and thinking “yes, that’s what I meant” feels exactly like understanding.

It is not the same thing. And from the inside, the two are indistinguishable.

The only way to tell them apart is to try to produce the answer yourself, from nothing — which is what you just spent twenty minutes discovering.

An examlet only ever asks you to produce.

Use the tools

You are allowed to use AI in this course, on homework, assignments, and labs. I mean that.

But what it hands you is a hypothesis until you can produce it yourself.

The check that costs you nothing

Before you leave: three words

Your group got somewhere none of you got alone. That happened because somebody in your group refused to accept a vague sentence.

So here is the contract. The three of you agree that from now on, when one of you says something fuzzy, any of the others gets to say:

“Say that more precisely.”

No apology needed. It is not rude. It is the most useful thing you can say to someone who is trying to learn this material.

You will hear me say it all term.

This is the durable takeaway – a shared phrase costs nothing, requires no exchange of anything personal, and gives every student permission to push back on vagueness without it feeling like an attack.

Then start using it yourself, constantly, from Monday on. If they hear it in week 1 and then hear it every week, it stops being an event and becomes how this course talks.
